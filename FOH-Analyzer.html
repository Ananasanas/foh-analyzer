        What to listen for

        RangeCharacterUsual suspectsWhat to do
        20–60 HzSub / rumbleKick and synth subs, stage rumble, wind and handling noise, HVACHigh-pass every channel that does not need it (vocals 80–120 Hz, guitars 80–100 Hz). Keep sub level and sub-to-main alignment in check before reaching for EQ.
        60–125 HzWeight / boomKick fundamental, bass guitar, room modesRoom modes show up as narrow, steady bumps that do not follow the music. Cut them narrow on the system EQ. Let kick and bass take turns: cut one where the other lives.
        125–250 HzWarmth / proximityClose-miked vocals, toms, acoustic guitar bodyProximity effect builds up when singers eat the mic. Use the channel HPF and a gentle low-shelf cut before cutting the master.
        250–500 HzMudMany open mics summing, stage wash, keys and guitar body, tomsThe most common live build-up. Cut on the sources (kick 300–400, toms 300–500, acoustic 200–300), gate or duck idle mics, then 2–3 dB on the bus if needed.
        500–1k HzBoxy / hollowSnare shell, kick beater box, cheap DI'd keysNarrow cuts on the channel. Avoid wide master cuts here: it hollows out vocals.
        1–2 kHzHonk / nasalHorns, distorted guitar, telephone-like vocalsFind the source with a sweep. A small 1.2–1.6 kHz cut on guitars often clears room for the vocal.
        2–5 kHzPresence / harshnessVocals, cymbals, guitar fizz, horn-loaded PAEars are most sensitive here, so it tires an audience fastest. Use dynamic EQ on the vocal bus around 2.5–4 kHz. Also the most feedback-prone band for vocal mics and wedges.
        5–10 kHzSibilance / brillianceEsses, hi-hats, snare wires, acoustic guitar stringsDe-ess vocals at 5–8 kHz. Hats that cut through 10 m away are too loud for the front row.
        10–20 kHzAirCymbals, vocal breath, PA HF sectionAir drops with distance and humidity. A shelf on the system tunes the far field. Do not chase it at FOH if the front fills are already bright.
        Narrow peaksRinging / feedbackVocal mics in front of wedges or mains, lav mics, acoustic instrumentsA ring stays at one pitch while the music moves. Pull the fader first, then cut 3–6 dB with a narrow filter (Q 8–10) at the marked frequency. Ring out wedges before doors open.
        Shortcuts: Space freeze · P reset peaks · R capture reference · V RTA / FFT. Levels read as dB SPL once you calibrate; until then they are relative to an assumed offset.

        Mic calibration

        The mic is not running.
        1. Fit the calibrator snugly over the mic capsule, with the right adapter for the capsule size.
        2. Switch it on and keep your hands off the mic while the reading settles.
        3. When all four checks are green the offset is set, or press Set calibration.

        1. Put a trusted SPL meter right next to the measurement mic, pointing the same way.
        2. Play steady pink noise at 85–100 dB. Set the meter to the same weighting and time below.
        3. Type the meter's reading and press Match.
        --dB
        This program, 2 s average

        Type a known offset, for example one written down from an earlier calibration of the same mic and interface gain. Reading = 10·log10(mean square) + offset.

        Load the calibration file that came with your measurement mic: miniDSP UMIK, Dayton UMM/EMM, Sonarworks, Earthworks and similar plain-text files with a frequency and a dB value on each line. The program inverts the capsule's response and corrects the RTA, FFT and SPL meter with it. The file is saved for this mic.

        A software calibrator. Inside the program feeds a perfect tone straight into the analyzer at the level you pick, to check the meter, weightings and RTA. Through speakers plays the tone out loud so you can check that the mic and interface respond. Neither replaces a real calibrator: software cannot know the true SPL coming out of a speaker.
